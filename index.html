Dissertation

Current Work: Search-Distilled Dispatch for Demand-Responsive Transit: A Compute–Quality Frontier

Hyun Kim, Branislav Dimitrijevic, Mohammadjavad Bazdar
Under review at Transportation Research Part C: Emerging Technologies (submitted November 2026)

Small demand-responsive fleets are judged against contractual limits on passenger wait and detour. The dispatch methods that protect those limits are either fast heuristics or optimizers whose quality depends on how much computation each decision is allowed. This work asks how much of a search planner's quality a learned policy keeps at a millisecond budget, and where on the compute axis an optimizer overtakes it.

Approach

  • A beam-search planner with a guard-first objective serves as the teacher
  • Its decisions are distilled into a neural policy by behavior cloning and expert iteration
  • The policy is then improved by proximal policy optimization held on a leash to the cloned policy
  • Evaluation is on paired seeds against nearest-first dispatch, two insertion heuristics, the teacher, and a rolling-horizon re-optimizer whose time limit is swept from tens of milliseconds to seconds
  • Demand is synthetic, replayed New York taxi trips, and both under stochastic travel times

Findings

  • The policy decides in 5 ms on one CPU thread
  • At the re-optimizer's smallest time limit, the policy is better on most wait metrics
  • The re-optimizer matches the policy at five to fifty times its compute, depending on demand, and at about two hundred times wins the wait and ride tails
  • Under travel-time noise on real demand, the re-optimizer and insertion degrade more than the policy
  • Three negative results are reported, including that prolonged fine-tuning sacrifices whichever service axis the reward leaves unpriced

Evaluation code, trained policy weights and results will be released when the paper is published.

Dissertation Proposal - Learning-Based Single Agent Routing Under Stochastic Demand

Published in Urban Science, 10(5), 287 (2026) — DOI: 10.3390/urbansci10050287

To ensure feasibility and computational tractability during the dissertation proposal stage, several modeling assumptions were intentionally relaxed to enable development of a functional prototype. The objective was to validate the learning framework before introducing full real-world complexity.

  • Simplified urban environment representation without full dynamic congestion modeling
  • Reduced time-dependent components (e.g., simplified stochastic travel times and time-window constraints)
  • Controlled passenger generation mechanisms to limit combinatorial routing complexity
  • Simplified shuttle capacity and bundling logic to stabilize early-stage learning
  • Use of Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL) to bootstrap policy learning prior to full RL optimization

These structured relaxations allowed the reinforcement learning framework to converge reliably and demonstrate proof-of-concept viability. The prototype establishes a scalable foundation for progressively reintroducing realistic operational constraints, including dynamic congestion, richer demand patterns, and advanced multi-agent coordination.

Distributed Reinforcement Learning for Multi-Agent Shuttle Routing

Independent doctoral research focused on scalable reinforcement learning for shared autonomous mobility systems.

I designed and deployed an on-premise distributed reinforcement learning cluster using Ray, consisting of seven nodes (six workers and one learner). The system is fully containerized with Docker and supports GPU-accelerated training via CUDA. Both rollout workers and the central learner leverage GPUs for efficient forward inference and backpropagation. The cluster currently sustains approximately 2,000 concurrent custom simulation environments for high-throughput policy learning.

This infrastructure supports my dissertation, tentatively titled: “Reinforcement Learning for Multi-Agent Shuttle Routing Optimization.”

  • A custom simulator of pooled shuttle dispatch with stochastic demand, ride-sharing constraints and replayed New York taxi trips
  • A beam-search planner used as the teacher for imitation learning
  • Policy distillation by behavior cloning and expert iteration, followed by PPO fine-tuning
  • Paired-seed benchmarking against insertion heuristics and a rolling-horizon re-optimizer across compute budgets

The latest results are in the manuscript under review at Transportation Research Part C (see the Dissertation tab). Earlier stages of the work trained multi-agent PPO with recurrent policies directly; the analysis of why that approach stalled is one of the two papers under review for the TRB 2027 Annual Meeting.

NSF IRES

NSF IRES: AI in Smart Transportation (KAIST, South Korea)

NSF International Research Experience for Students (IRES) — US–Korea Advanced Transportation Infrastructure Informatics Institute (ATI3)

I was selected as an NSF IRES Fellow and participated in an intensive three-week international research program hosted by the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST). The program immersed students in applied artificial intelligence and simulation for intelligent transportation systems through lectures, hands-on labs, field visits, and research-driven team projects. Core topics included machine learning fundamentals (regression/classification, RNN/LSTM, reinforcement learning), AI-based perception and analytics (e.g., object detection and trajectory tracking), and mixed-mobility traffic simulation using SUMO.

A key component of the program was collaborative, transdisciplinary research conducted in joint US–Korea teams (typically two students from the U.S. paired with two KAIST students). Teams developed an AI + transportation research study, presented findings at the end of the program, and produced a final paper each year to document methods, results, and implications for smart mobility. The program also featured guest lectures from leading transportation and technology practitioners—including senior industry leaders such as the CTO of Kakao Mobility—providing exposure to real-world deployment challenges and emerging trends in smart transportation.

My 2024 NSF IRES team paper was subsequently selected for a lecture presentation at the 2025 Transportation Research Board (TRB) Annual Meeting, highlighting the program’s emphasis on producing publication-quality, conference-ready research.

Program highlights include structured mentoring, professional development, and industry engagement within Korea’s smart mobility ecosystem.

Competitions

Transportation Technology Tournament (TTT)

Hosted by the National Operations Center of Excellence (NOCoE) and the Institute of Transportation Engineers (ITE)

The Transportation Technology Tournament (TTT) is an annual national student competition that connects multidisciplinary teams with public transportation agencies to solve real-world mobility and operations challenges using Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) and Transportation Systems Management & Operations (TSMO) concepts.

Teams of 4–6 students collaborate with agency partners and academic mentors to develop solution concepts (light ConOps), supported by virtual technical training and industry guidance. Finalist teams are invited to present their work at the ITE Annual Meeting, with travel expenses covered. Typical projects leverage emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence, machine learning, computer vision, digital twins, and traffic analytics.

The competition emphasizes applied problem-solving, professional communication, and workforce development while providing agencies with innovative, student-driven approaches to operational transportation challenges.
